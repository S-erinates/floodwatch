📡 How Does Radar See Water?

Unlike optical sensors, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) transmits its own microwave pulses, operating day and night through heavy clouds and rain. Calm water surfaces act like a mirror (specular reflection), bouncing radar energy away from the satellite and appearing very dark (low backscatter in dB). HH polarization is highly sensitive to surface roughness and open water, while HV polarization responds to vegetation structure and flooded vegetation canopies.

🌍 Study Area & Methodology

Study Region: Dual-date NISAR GCOV scenes from the same track/frame and orbit direction were selected via ASF Vertex to ensure co-registered grid alignment.

Methodology: `HHHH` and `HVHV` backscatter grids under `science/LSAR/GCOV/grids/frequencyA/` were extracted using `h5py`, converted to decibels (`10 * log10`), smoothed with a speckle filter, and classified into binary water masks (~ -19 dB / Otsu threshold) to compute total water area (km²) and net percentage change.

📊 Findings, Limitations & Future Applications

Interpretation: Observed changes in surface water extent correlate with regional precipitation events, floodplain inundation, seasonal hydrology, or reservoir management.

Limitations: This rapid analysis relies on dual-date thresholding. While SAR is unaffected by cloud cover, strong winds can roughen open water surfaces (increasing backscatter and affecting water mask accuracy), and dense forest canopies can induce double-bounce scattering.

Future Impact: Leveraging NISAR's 12-day repeat cycle, FloodWatch can scale into an automated early flood warning tool, drought/reservoir tracker, and agricultural water management system.

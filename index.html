RADAR PHYSICS

📡 How Does Radar See Water?

Unlike optical satellites blinded by cloud cover or night, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) transmits active L-band microwave pulses (~24 cm wavelength). Calm water surfaces act as a mirror (specular reflection), deflecting radar energy away from the sensor so water pixels appear extremely dark (low dB). HH polarization captures surface roughness variations, while HV cross-polarization penetrates vegetation to detect flooded forests and agricultural wetlands.

REGIONAL CONTEXT & PIPELINE

🌍 Study Area & Methodology

Study Area: Co-registered dual-date NISAR GCOV scenes (matching track, frame, and orbit direction) were retrieved via ASF Vertex.

Processing Pipeline: Using Python (`h5py`), `HHHH` and `HVHV` covariance grids under `science/LSAR/GCOV/grids/frequencyA/` were converted to decibels (`10 * log10`), filtered for speckle noise, and segmented via Otsu thresholding (~ -19 dB) to quantify exact water surface area (km²) and net change.

INTERPRETATION & ROADMAP

📊 Hydrological Findings, Limitations & Future Impact

Hydrological Drivers

Observed surface water expansion/recession aligns with extreme precipitation events, floodplain overflow, seasonal snowmelt, or dam reservoir release operations.

Scientific Limitations

Single-threshold binary classification across two dates can be influenced by wind-induced capillary waves (which roughen water and raise dB backscatter) or double-bounce scattering in dense flooded forests.

NISAR Future Applications

With NISAR's systematic 12-day global repeat cycle, FloodWatch scales into an operational early flood warning system, drought reservoir monitor, and precision irrigation planner.
